CHAPTER 1

Introduction to Computers and Computer Systems


1.1 Introduction of Computer

A computer is an advanced electronic device that accepts raw data as input, stores and processes that data according to a set of programmed instructions, and produces meaningful information as output. Modern society relies heavily on computing infrastructure for everything from global telecommunications to desktop automation.

1.1.1 Definition, Characteristics, and Applications of Computer

At its foundation, a computer functions under the Input-Process-Output (IPO) model. Raw data (facts, numbers, symbols) enters the machine, undergoes algorithmic manipulation in the central processor, and is converted into structured information usable by humans or other automated systems.

Core Characteristics of Computers:

  • Speed: Computers perform calculations in fractions of a second (nanoseconds and picoseconds). For example, a modern multi-GHz processor executes billions of instructions per second.
  • Accuracy: Computers execute tasks with high precision. Errors generally stem from human programming mistakes or incorrect input data (known as Garbage In, Garbage Out or GIGO).
  • Diligence: Unlike humans, computers do not suffer from physical tiredness, lack of concentration, or boredom. A computer can run repetitive tasks continuously for long durations without degrading operational performance.
  • Versatility: A single computer system can perform vastly different tasks sequentially or simultaneously—ranging from typing a text document to rendering 3D graphics or analyzing weather patterns.
  • Storage Capacity: Computers store massive volumes of data in compact spaces, using primary volatile memory and non-volatile secondary storage media for quick retrieval.
  • Automation: Once a program is loaded into memory, a computer can execute its instructions sequentially without requiring manual human intervention at every step.

Key Application Domains:

  • Education: Powering E-learning platforms, virtual classrooms, digital libraries, and academic research database tools.
  • Healthcare: Operating diagnostic imaging equipment (CT scanners, MRI machines), patient monitoring software, and electronic medical health records.
  • Banking & Finance: Managing Core Banking Systems (CBS), handling global electronic fund transfers (SWIFT), running Automated Teller Machines (ATMs), and executing algorithmic stock trading.
  • Science & Engineering: Running Computer-Aided Design (CAD) software for architectural modeling, space exploration simulations, and molecular biology analysis.
  • Business & Industry: Automating Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), inventory management, digital point-of-sale billing, and corporate payroll processing.

1.1.2 Evolution of Computer Technology

The history of computing spans thousands of years, starting with mechanical counting tools like the Abacus (developed around 2500 BCE) and progressing through mechanical calculators like Blaise Pascal's Pascaline (1642) and Charles Babbage's Difference Engine and Analytical Engine in the 19th century. Babbage's Analytical Engine introduced concepts of input, processing, memory, and output, earning him the title of the "Father of Computing."

Modern electronic computing is divided into five distinct generations based on underlying hardware technology:

Generation Hardware Technology & Key Features Examples
First Generation
(1940–1956)		 Vacuum Tubes: Utilized fragile, heat-producing glass vacuum tubes for circuitry and magnetic drums for memory. Massive size, huge electricity consumption, programmed in machine language. ENIAC, UNIVAC-I, EDVAC
Second Generation
(1956–1963)		 Transistors: Replaced vacuum tubes with solid-state semiconductor transistors. Reduced size, lowered power consumption, increased speeds. Introduced Assembly language, FORTRAN, and COBOL. IBM 7094, CDC 1604, UNIVAC 1107
Third Generation
(1964–1971)		 Integrated Circuits (ICs): Combined hundreds of microscopic transistors onto a single silicon chip. Boosted speed, lowered costs. Introduced keyboards, monitors, and early operating systems. IBM System/360, PDP-8, Honeywell-6000
Fourth Generation
(1971–Present)		 Microprocessors (VLSI / ULSI): Placed millions/billions of transistors onto a single microchip (CPU). Launched personal computers (PCs), laptops, and mobile devices. Intel 8086/Pentium, Apple M-series, PCs
Fifth Generation
(Present & Beyond)		 AI & Quantum Computing: Parallel processing, neural networks, machine learning, natural language processing. Focuses on self-learning, pattern recognition, and autonomous decisions. AI supercomputer clusters, Quantum processors (Sycamore), Robotics

1.1.3 Measurement Unit of Processing Speed and Storage Unit

To evaluate system performance and capacity, computing relies on standardized metric units for data storage and execution speed.

Storage Measurement Units:

The basic unit of digital data is a Bit (Binary Digit), representing a single binary state of 0 or 1. Data storage scales upward in standardized byte multiples:

  • 1 Nibble = 4 bits
  • 1 Byte (B) = 8 bits (stores a single ASCII text character)
  • 1 Kilobyte (KB) = 1,024 Bytes (210 Bytes)
  • 1 Megabyte (MB) = 1,024 KB (220 Bytes)
  • 1 Gigabyte (GB) = 1,024 MB (230 Bytes)
  • 1 Terabyte (TB) = 1,024 GB (240 Bytes)
  • 1 Petabyte (PB) = 1,024 TB (250 Bytes)
  • 1 Exabyte (EB) = 1,024 PB (260 Bytes)

Processing Speed Measurement Units:

Processor execution rates were historically measured in instructions per second: MIPS (Millions of Instructions Per Second) or FLOPS (Floating-Point Operations Per Second). Modern CPU speed is primarily measured by internal clock frequency in Hertz (Hz):

  • Megahertz (MHz): Millions of clock cycles per second.
  • Gigahertz (GHz): Billions of clock cycles per second (e.g., a 3.5 GHz CPU completes 3.5 × 109 cycles per second).

1.1.4 Super, Mainframe, Mini, and Microcomputers

Computers are categorized into four major classes based on processing capacity, physical size, cost, and deployment scenarios:

  • Supercomputers: The fastest, largest, and most expensive computers available. Supercomputers use thousands of interconnected parallel processors to solve complex, compute-heavy scientific problems (measured in Petaflops or Exaflops).
    Examples: Frontier, Fugaku, Summit.
    Use Cases: Climate simulation, nuclear physics modeling, global weather forecasting.

  • Mainframe Computers: High-performance enterprise computers designed to process massive volumes of concurrent transactions and input/output requests reliably.
    Examples: IBM zSeries (z16), UNISYS ClearPath.
    Use Cases: Core banking transactions, airline reservations, national census processing.

  • Minicomputers (Midrange Computers): Multi-user systems scaled between personal microcomputers and mainframes, supporting dozens to hundreds of concurrent users.
    Examples: DEC VAX series, AS/400 (IBM System i).
    Use Cases: Industrial process automation, departmental databases.

  • Microcomputers: Single-user computers powered by a single microprocessor chip. Small, affordable, and common in everyday environments.
    Examples: Desktop PCs, Laptops, Workstations, Tablets.
    Use Cases: Document creation, web browsing, multimedia editing.

1.1.5 Mobile Computing and its Applications

Mobile computing is a technology framework that allows users to access, transmit, and process data wirelessly without being anchored to a fixed physical location. It combines mobile hardware, cellular/Wi-Fi networks, and specialized mobile operating systems.

Key Characteristics of Mobile Computing:

  • Portability: Lightweight hardware designed to be carried easily by hand or worn on the body.
  • Wireless Connectivity: Continuous network communication using radio frequencies without physical cables.
  • Location Awareness: Integrated Global Positioning System (GPS) chips enable location-based services and navigation.

Applications of Mobile Computing:

  • Mobile Banking (M-Banking): Real-time digital fund transfers and balance inquiries via mobile wallet apps (e.g., eSewa, Khalti, Google Pay).
  • Location-Based Navigation: On-demand transportation (e.g., Pathao, InDrive, Uber) and mapping services (e.g., Google Maps).
  • Mobile Commerce (M-Commerce): Purchasing goods via mobile storefront apps (e.g., Daraz, Amazon).
  • Remote Field Operations & Telemedicine: On-site data capture, real-time reports, and medical video consultations.

1.2 Computer System and I/O Devices

A computer system is an integrated assembly of hardware components and software applications designed to perform data processing tasks.

1.2.1 Concept of Computer Architecture and Organization

  • Computer Architecture: Refers to the logical and functional attributes of a computer system visible to a programmer, including instruction sets (ISA), data types, and register configurations.
    Example: Deciding whether a processor uses an x86 or ARM instruction set.

  • Computer Organization: Refers to the operational hardware units and physical interconnections that implement architectural specifications, focusing on signal timings, bus structures, and circuit layouts.
    Example: Designing a specific 3.2 GHz circuit board with a 12 MB L3 cache to execute an ARM instruction set.

The baseline model for modern general-purpose computing is the Von Neumann Architecture, where program instructions and data reside together in a shared central memory space fetched sequentially by the central processor.

1.2.2 Components of Computer System

  • Input Unit: Captures external data/commands and converts them into binary machine signals.
  • Output Unit: Translates processed binary data into human-readable text, graphics, sound, or physical actions.
  • Processing Unit (CPU): Executes software instructions, performs operations, and coordinates data flow.
  • Memory Unit (Primary Memory): High-speed internal semiconductor storage holding active code and data for quick access.
  • Storage Unit (Secondary Memory): High-capacity non-volatile storage media designed to preserve data long-term.

1.2.3 Microprocessor: Basic Concepts, Clock Speed, Word Length, Components, and Functions

A microprocessor is an integrated circuit built on a single semiconductor microchip that contains the complete Central Processing Unit (CPU).

Core Metrics:

  • Clock Speed: Synchronizes internal execution steps (measured in GHz). Higher clock speeds mean more instruction steps per second.
  • Word Length (Word Size): The maximum number of binary bits a microprocessor can process in a single parallel clock cycle (e.g., 32-bit vs. 64-bit).

Primary Internal Components:

  • Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU): Performs arithmetic calculations (+, -, *) and logical comparisons (AND, OR, NOT).
  • Control Unit (CU): Fetches instructions from main memory, decodes them into control signals, and directs data movement.
  • Register Array: Small, high-speed internal storage locations used to hold temporary data values and memory addresses during active execution cycles.

1.2.4 Bus System: Data Bus, Address Bus, and Control Bus

A bus system is a collection of parallel physical wires or circuit board traces used to transmit signals between the CPU, memory, and expansion units.

  • Data Bus (Bidirectional): Transfers actual binary data payload values back and forth between the CPU, RAM, and storage controllers.
  • Address Bus (Unidirectional): Driven exclusively by the CPU to select specific physical memory address locations or I/O ports.
  • Control Bus: Conveys command timing signals, system status checks, interrupt requests, and read/write execution commands.

1.2.5 Primary Memory: Definition, RAM, ROM, Cache, Buffer, Types of RAM and ROM

Primary Memory refers to semiconductor storage accessible directly by the CPU via the internal system bus.

Random Access Memory (RAM) - Volatile:

  • SRAM (Static RAM): Fast, uses flip-flop circuits, requires no refresh cycles, expensive; used in CPU cache memory.
  • DRAM (Dynamic RAM): Uses transistor-capacitor pairs, requires periodic electrical refreshing; used for main memory modules (DDR4, DDR5).

Read-Only Memory (ROM) - Non-Volatile:

  • PROM (Programmable ROM): Blank ROM chips that can be programmed once using high voltage.
  • EPROM (Erasable Programmable ROM): Contents can be erased using intense ultraviolet (UV) light.
  • EEPROM (Electrically Erasable Programmable ROM): Modern non-volatile memory erased and rewritten electrically byte-by-byte (used in flash BIOS chips).

Cache Memory & Buffer:

  • Cache Memory: Extremely fast SRAM situated close to or inside the CPU die (L1, L2, L3 caches) to store frequently accessed data.
    Analogy: Think of RAM as a bookshelf in your office, while Cache is the stack of papers sitting directly on your desk for immediate reference.

  • Data Buffer: Temporary block of physical memory reserved to hold data while transferring between devices operating at different speeds.

1.2.6 Secondary Memory

  • Magnetic Disk (HDD): Rotating platters with magnetic coating. Offers high capacity at low cost, but sensitive to physical shock.
  • Flash Memory (SSD): Solid-state non-volatile storage using floating-gate NAND circuits with no moving parts.
  • Optical Disks: Circular plastic disks read by laser diodes.
    • CD: ~700 MB capacity
    • DVD: ~4.7 GB to 8.5 GB capacity
    • Blu-ray Disc (BD): 25 GB to 100+ GB capacity
  • External Storage & USB Flash Drives: Portable enclosures or thumb-sized flash memory drives connected via USB/Thunderbolt.

1.2.7 Input Devices

Devices that convert human input or environmental data into binary signals:

  • Keyboard: Alphanumeric key matrix interface.
  • Mouse: Hand-held pointing device with optical movement sensor.
  • Scanner: Converts physical documents into bitmap image files.
  • Light Pen: Light-sensitive stylus for detecting display screen positioning.
  • OMR (Optical Mark Reader): Detects light reflection off marked paper areas (multiple-choice exams).
  • OCR (Optical Character Recognition): Converts printed/handwritten text images into editable text files.
  • BCR (Barcode Reader): Reads black-and-white optical barcode patterns.
  • MICR (Magnetic Ink Character Recognition): Reads characters printed with magnetic iron-oxide ink (bank checks).
  • Touch Screen: Displays overlaid with capacitive/resistive touch-sensing panels.
  • Microphone: Acoustic transducer converting sound waves to digitized audio.
  • Digital Camera: Focuses light onto electronic sensors (CCD/CMOS arrays) to record media.

1.2.8 Output Devices

1. Display Monitors (Soft-Copy Output)

  • LCD (Liquid Crystal Display): Uses liquid crystals between polarizing glass plates illuminated by CCFL backlights.
  • LED (Light Emitting Diode): Uses energy-efficient LEDs for backlighting, offering higher contrast and thinner profiles.

2. Printers (Hard-Copy Output)

  • Dot Matrix Printer (Impact): Metal pins strike an inked ribbon to form text from dots; useful for multi-part carbon forms.
  • Inkjet Printer (Non-Impact): Sprays microscopic drops of liquid ink onto paper.
  • Laser Printer (Non-Impact): Uses electrostatic drums, laser beams, and powdered toner fused with hot rollers for sharp prints.

3. Speakers (Audio Output)

  • Speakers: Transducers converting digital/analog electrical signals into physical sound waves.

1.2.9 Hardware Interfaces

  • Parallel Port (DB-25): Legacy 25-pin interface transferring 8 bits simultaneously over parallel wires (printers).
  • Serial Port (RS-232 / DB-9): Legacy 9-pin interface transferring data sequentially 1 bit at a time over longer distances.
  • USB Ports: Universal plug-and-play interface for power and data delivery (Type-A, Type-B, Type-C).
  • HDMI: All-digital cable interface transmitting uncompressed HD video and multi-channel audio over a single cable.
  • Expansion Slots (PCIe): High-speed motherboard sockets used to install internal cards like GPUs, network adapters, and NVMe SSD expansions.
