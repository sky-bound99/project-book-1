CHAPTER 8

Cyber Law, Computer Ethics, and Cybersecurity

8.1 Digital Society and Computer Ethics

A digital society is a modern social structure driven by the pervasive integration of computer networks, information technology, and high-speed digital communication into everyday personal, economic, and governmental activities. In a digital society, data serves as a vital economic asset, enabling global commerce, digital education, remote healthcare, and real-time social connection. However, this global connectivity introduces complex moral and legal dilemmas regarding how technology should be developed, deployed, and used responsibly.

Computer ethics defines the set of moral principles, standards, and behavioral guidelines governing the responsible use of computer systems, software applications, data assets, and network resources. Ethical issues in computing center around user data privacy, intellectual property theft, digital surveillance, algorithmic bias, and the societal impact of artificial intelligence automation. Major computer crimes involve unauthorized system access (hacking), online identity theft, digital fraud, software piracy, network sabotage, and malicious harassment. To maintain order and trust within a digital society, institutions deploy technical, legal, and operational security measures, including strong identity authentication controls, access permissions, network traffic monitoring, and formal ethical codes of conduct enforced across professional computing organizations.

8.2 Concept of Information Security

Information security encompasses the technologies, policies, operational strategies, and management practices designed to protect digital data assets and computing systems from unauthorized access, disclosure, modification, disruption, or destruction. The foundational architecture of information security is defined by the CIA Triad model:

  • Confidentiality: Ensuring that sensitive information is accessible exclusively to authorized individuals and systems, preventing unauthorized data leaks or interception.
  • Integrity: Safeguarding the accuracy, completeness, and authenticity of data, ensuring that information is protected against unauthorized tampering, modification, or deletion.
  • Availability: Guaranteeing that critical system resources, services, and data repositories remain operational and accessible to authorized users whenever needed.

Threats to information security originate from human actors, software vulnerabilities, and physical disasters. Physical threats include equipment theft, hardware failures, fire, and power outages. Human threats include social engineering tactics, unauthorized access, and insider data theft. Digital network threats include malware infections, man-in-the-middle interception attacks, and Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) campaigns designed to overload web infrastructure.

Data protection relies heavily on cryptography—the mathematical science of securing data during transmission and storage. Encryption is the process of converting readable plain-text data into an unreadable, scrambled format called ciphertext using a mathematical algorithm and a secret key. Decryption is the reverse process, transforming unreadable ciphertext back into its original readable plain-text format using a corresponding key. Modern cryptography operates under two primary models: Symmetric Encryption, which uses a single shared secret key for both encryption and decryption (such as AES), and Asymmetric Encryption, which uses a mathematically linked key pair consisting of a public key for encryption and a private key for decryption (such as RSA).

8.3 Concept of Cybercrime

Cybercrime refers to any illegal activity executed using computer systems, network devices, or digital infrastructure, either as a primary tool to commit an offense or as the target of the crime itself. Unlike traditional physical crimes bounded by geographical borders, cybercrimes are committed across digital networks, allowing perpetrators to execute attacks remotely while hiding behind digital anonymity.

Cybercrimes cover a broad spectrum of malicious activities. Hacking involves exploiting hardware or software vulnerabilities to gain unauthorized entry into secure systems. Online financial fraud and identity theft manipulate personal credentials to steal funds or impersonate victims. Cyberstalking and harassment use online channels to threaten, intimidate, or defame individuals continuously. Digital extortion campaigns, such as ransomware attacks, lock critical corporate data files until victims pay ransom fees. Other offenses include digital intellectual property piracy, database tampering, and nation-state cyber warfare operations targeting critical public infrastructure.

To combat digital offenses, nations enact comprehensive cyber laws (cyber legislation). Cyber laws establish legal frameworks that define criminal offenses executed in digital spaces, specify investigative protocols for law enforcement agencies, mandate strict evidentiary standards for digital forensics evidence submitted in judicial courts, and impose punitive fines or imprisonment terms on offenders. These legal frameworks deter cybercriminals, safeguard consumer trust in online commerce, and establish international legal cooperation channels to extradite cyber offenders across international boundaries.

8.4 Malicious Software and Spam

Malicious software, universally abbreviated as malware, is an umbrella term referring to any intrusive software program or computer code intentionally designed to compromise system security, disrupt device hardware, steal private data, or gain unauthorized access to computer networks without user consent.

Malware exists in several distinct categories based on propagation mechanisms and operational targets:

  • Viruses: Malicious code blocks that attach to legitimate executable files or host documents. Viruses require human interaction (such as running an infected file) to execute and spread across systems.
  • Worms: Self-replicating, standalone malicious programs that automatically spread across local area networks and internet connections by exploiting system vulnerabilities without requiring human intervention or host file execution.
  • Trojans (Trojan Horses): Deceptive programs that masquerade as legitimate, useful software (such as free utilities or games). Once installed by the user, they drop malicious payloads, open remote backdoor entry points, or log user keystrokes.
  • Ransomware: Highly destructive malware that encrypts a victim's personal data files or locks the operating system, demanding cryptocurrency ransom payments in exchange for decryption keys.
  • Spyware and Keyloggers: Covert monitoring programs that track user web browsing habits, harvest credit card numbers, and capture typed keystrokes to transmit credentials to external servers secretly.

Phishing is a social engineering attack where malicious actors impersonate legitimate entities—such as banks, corporate IT departments, or trusted service providers—via deceptive emails, messages, or fraudulent websites to trick users into revealing sensitive credentials, passwords, or credit card details. Spam refers to unsolicited, mass-distributed electronic communications sent over email, messaging apps, or forums, often carrying commercial advertisements, fraudulent scams, or embedded phishing links.

Preventing malware infections and spam requires comprehensive technical and operational safeguards: installing reputable antivirus and anti-malware software engines, enabling automated operating system security patches, deploying intelligent network email spam filters, adopting strict password hygiene paired with Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA), and conducting security awareness training to educate users on identifying suspicious link attachments and fraudulent email headers.

8.5 Protection from Cybercrime

Defending computing infrastructure against cybercrime requires a multi-layered security strategy—often referred to as defense-in-depth—that combines physical security controls, perimeter network defenses, endpoint protection applications, and rigorous data backup procedures.

A firewall is a network security hardware appliance or software module that inspects, filters, and controls incoming and outgoing network traffic based on predetermined security rules. Operating as a protective gateway between a trusted internal network and untrusted external networks (such as the public internet), firewalls analyze data packet headers to block unauthorized connections, drop suspicious traffic, and prevent malicious network access attempts.

Antivirus software is an endpoint protection program designed to detect, prevent, quarantine, and remove malicious software programs from host computer systems. Modern antivirus platforms rely on signature-based detection (matching file hashes against known malware databases), heuristic analysis (identifying suspicious behavioral patterns in unknown code), and real-time cloud threat intelligence to neutralize emerging zero-day vulnerabilities before they execute.

Data backup is the process of creating duplicate copies of critical data files and database systems, storing them on separate physical or cloud storage media. Backups serve as an essential safety net against data loss caused by hardware crashes, physical disasters, accidental deletion, or ransomware infections. Organizations adhere to the established 3-2-1 backup strategy: maintain at least three total copies of important data, store the backup copies across two different types of media (such as local external drives and network storage), and keep at least one backup copy completely off-site or within secure cloud repositories.

8.6 Intellectual Property Rights

Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) are legal rights granted to individuals, inventors, and corporate organizations over original creations of the human mind, including literary works, artistic expressions, software source code, industrial inventions, brand logos, and commercial designs. In a digital society where digital files can be duplicated perfectly and distributed globally at zero marginal cost, robust intellectual property rights protect creators' investments, encouraging technical innovation and creative output.

Key legal frameworks governing intellectual property include:

  • Copyright: A legal right granting the creator of an original work exclusive authority to publish, reproduce, distribute, display, perform, or license that work. In computing, copyright laws protect computer software source code, structural documentation, digital artwork, website contents, and database structures against unauthorized copying or commercial piracy.
  • Patents: Exclusive property rights granted by government authorities for a specific invention, technical process, or unique algorithmic solution. A patent grants the inventor exclusive rights to manufacture, sell, license, or use the technological process for a limited period (typically twenty years), preventing competitors from commercializing the patented technology without permission.
  • Trademarks: Distinctive signs, symbols, logos, names, phrases, or visual designs used by commercial entities to identify and distinguish their products or services from competitors in the market. Trademarks prevent brand confusion, protecting corporate identity and reputation in digital domain names, software brands, and commercial services.

8.7 Concept of Digital Signature

A Digital Signature is a mathematical cryptographic mechanism used to verify the authenticity, integrity, and non-repudiation of a digital document, software package, or electronic message. Functioning as the legal digital equivalent of a handwritten signature, a digital signature proves to the recipient that a message was genuinely created by the claimed sender and that its contents were not altered during network transit.

Digital signatures operate using Public Key Cryptography (Asymmetric Encryption) combined with cryptographic hash functions:

  1. Signing Process: The sender takes a digital document and processes it through a cryptographic hash algorithm (such as SHA-256) to produce a unique, fixed-length string of numbers called a message digest or hash code. The sender then encrypts this generated hash code using their private key. The resulting encrypted hash value is attached to the document as the digital signature.
  2. Verification Process: When the recipient receives the signed document, they decrypt the digital signature using the sender's publicly available public key to reveal the original hash code. Simultaneously, the recipient recalculates a fresh hash code from the received document text. If the decrypted hash matches the newly calculated hash code exactly, it proves two critical facts: the document was signed by the holder of the corresponding private key (authenticity), and the document content was not altered by a single character during transmission (integrity).

Furthermore, digital signatures enforce non-repudiation, meaning the signing party cannot deny having signed or sent the document, as the signature could only have been generated using their private key, which is kept secret by the owner.

8.8 Concept of Cyber Law in Nepal

In Nepal, the legal framework governing electronic transactions, digital records, cybersecurity offenses, and cybercrime prosecution is codified primarily under the Electronic Transaction Act (ETA), 2063 (2006 AD), often referred to as the Cyber Law of Nepal. Enacted to address the rapid adoption of information technology, the ETA provides legal recognition to electronic data, digital signatures, and online financial transactions while defining penalties for computer crimes committed within the country.

Key provisions and legal offenses regulated under the Electronic Transaction Act 2063 include:

  • Legalization of Electronic Records and Digital Signatures: The law legalizes electronic documents, digital signatures, and online communications, giving them equal legal status to traditional physical paperwork in government filings, commercial contracts, and judicial proceedings.
  • Hacking and System Tampering: Section 44 and Section 45 penalize unauthorized access, destruction, alteration, or disruption of computer systems, source code, and data networks with fines and imprisonment.
  • Online Defamation and Cyber Harassment: Section 47 strictly prohibits publishing or distributing illegal, obscene, or defamatory materials across electronic media, social networks, and websites that violate public morality, insult social harmony, or defame individuals, prescribing fines up to 100,000 Nepalese Rupees and imprisonment terms up to five years.
  • Computer Fraud and Data Theft: The law criminalizes unauthorized extraction, copying, or theft of corporate databases, bank records, and confidential government information.
  • Establishment of Adjudicating Bodies: The Act mandates the creation of specialized judicial entities, including the Information Technology Tribunal and the IT Appellate Tribunal, to adjudicate cybercrime cases, resolve digital signature disputes, and manage appeals efficiently.

8.9 ICT Policy in Nepal

The Information and Communication Technology (ICT) Policy of Nepal is a national strategic framework formulated by the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology to leverage technology for socioeconomic development, poverty reduction, good governance, and digital inclusion across the nation.

The core objectives and strategic focus areas of Nepal's national ICT Policy include:

  • Infrastructure Development: Expanding reliable broadband internet connectivity, optical fiber backbones, and 4G/5G mobile telecommunication networks across remote rural communities and mountainous districts, bridging the domestic digital divide.
  • E-Governance Implementation: Modernizing public service delivery by digitizing government operations, establishing national data centers, implementing central digital identity systems, and launching integrated public service portals to ensure transparent, accountable administrative governance.
  • Human Resource Capacity Building: Enhancing digital literacy across educational institutions, integrating computer science curricula into school systems, and promoting specialized vocational training programs to develop a skilled ICT workforce capable of supporting domestic tech industries.
  • Fostering ICT Industry and Innovation: Supporting domestic software development enterprises, technology startups, business process outsourcing (BPO) centers, and IT hubs through financial incentives, research grants, and public-private partnerships.
  • Cybersecurity and Legal Compliance: Strengthening national cybersecurity defenses, establishing a national Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT/CSIRT) to monitor network threats, and periodically updating cyber legal frameworks to protect critical national infrastructure against emerging digital threats.
